# Field notes from the Aeon system

## The promise ring

Meridian pilots have an old saying: “The ring is a promise, not permission.”

A relativistic drive begins with the destination, not the engine. The navigation solution measures a clear corridor and reserves a braking point. Only then do the drive coils charge. On the flight display, that agreement takes the shape of a circle around the nose marker. Hold the ship steady and the circle fills; turn away and the promise is withdrawn.

The first Nomad crews called it the promise ring. Kestrel pilots kept the name, despite insisting that sentiment had no place in an interceptor cockpit.

An ordinary approach ends twenty kilometres short: enough space to identify a station, inspect a landing site, or discover that a contract has omitted something with guns. Stellar observation uses a much wider stand-off. As the Meridian flight manual puts it, a drive may negotiate with inertia; it cannot negotiate with a photosphere.

The final command always belongs to the pilot. The ring can be full for an hour and the ship will stay where it is. Press the drive control and the corridor becomes a journey—up to nine tenths of light speed, followed by a very deliberate stop.

*In-world flavour for the current navigation system; this is fictional drive physics.*
